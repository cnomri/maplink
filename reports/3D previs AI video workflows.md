# Start with shots, graduate to gray boxes

The "previs-first agent workflow" in your pasted summary is real, and most of its specific claims hold up. Topview 3D Builder, Microsoft Research's Code Video Model, Scenario's Blender skills, ATAS, MotionBricks and the "code world model" projects all exist. The core ones share one idea: a coding agent builds a cheap, editable gray-box 3D scene that fixes *where* everything is and *how the camera moves*, and a video model then paints *how it looks*. The summary oversells three things. Topview's mannequins cannot move their bodies. "Scenario 3D OS" with "~80 skills" appears only in a third-party post published the day before this research. MotionBricks is a character-motion model released in April, and nothing documents the Gemini pairing the summary mentions. The bigger gap is between the research and the products you can use. Code Video Model's key trick is to let the video model see the gray box only during the first 5 of its 20 generation steps, so it keeps the layout without copying the flat gray look. That needs direct access to the model's internals, a roughly 210 GB checkpoint and more than one GPU. The consumer tools instead upload the gray-box clip to hosted "reference video" modes such as Seedance's. Those modes offer no such control, so whether the model follows the layout comes down to prompting. Calling this "the state of the art for controllable video" is premature: the consumer tools are weeks old, and nobody has published a controlled comparison against simpler methods. It is not the best place for you to start. Begin with a hosted generator (Kling 3.0 or Seedance) driven by reference images, and edit in DaVinci Resolve with real sound. Add previs, starting with Topview because it is free and runs on your machine, once your shots keep failing because of *where* things are rather than *how they look*. Make the MacBook your main machine if it has an Apple Silicon chip. The Windows PC earns a role only if it has an NVIDIA graphics card with 16 GB or more of video memory (VRAM), and then as a render box for open-source video models.

Several teams converged on "build a gray box, then render it" within days of each other. Code Video Model's last commits are dated Oct 12–13, Topview launched Oct 15, and Scenario added its Blender blockout skill on Oct 17 ([Scenario changelog](https://github.com/scenario-labs/skills/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md)). But Topview has **268 stars and zero open issues**, no Reddit or Hacker News threads turned up, and no third party has published a side-by-side test of how faithfully the video follows the blockout ([GitHub repo](https://github.com/topviewai/topview-3d-builder)). The fair description is "the leading edge for precise spatial control." It is not yet a settled best practice.

## Consumer tools fix the layout in 3D and leave the look to the video model

Topview states the division of labor directly: "The 3D scene fixes blocking, scale and camera angles. Texture, lighting and performance come from the video model" ([README](https://github.com/topviewai/topview-3d-builder/blob/main/README.md)). Everything else about these tools follows from that split.

### Topview's agent edits a scene file, measures it and checks snapshots

Topview 3D Builder is a skill for your AI agent built around a Python command-line tool (`topview-3d-cli`). It renders through an invisible browser (Chromium) and includes a local browser-based editor called Studio. You don't drag objects around; you chat. The agent turns "a man sits on a box, camera at eye level" into small JSON edit batches and runs them through the CLI. Each edit either applies completely or not at all, and every command returns JSON ([CLI reference](https://github.com/topviewai/topview-3d-builder/blob/main/docs/topview-3d-cli.md)). The agent then runs numerical checks: is the subject framed, is it inside the camera's view, does anything sink through the floor. It renders PNG frames and is told to look at them and fix problems. The work follows a **ten-stage workflow** (Understand, Layout, Props, People, Pose, Cameras, Edit, Check, Studio, Generate) and pauses for your approval between stages ([SKILL.md](https://github.com/topviewai/topview-3d-builder/blob/main/skills/topview-3d-cli/SKILL.md)). Scenes are measured in metres, every scene keeps a bird's-eye overview camera, and the default timeline is 24 fps for 120 frames. Studio opens at `127.0.0.1:3002`, and every agent operation has a matching manual panel, so you can reposition a character yourself ([README](https://github.com/topviewai/topview-3d-builder/blob/main/README.md)). You can import your own `.glb`/`.gltf` models, but FBX and Mixamo are not documented. The agent is told to build props from one to four primitive shapes first, because "a phone can be a slab" ([add-prop.md](https://github.com/topviewai/topview-3d-builder/blob/main/skills/topview-3d-cli/references/add-prop.md), [SKILL.md](https://github.com/topviewai/topview-3d-builder/blob/main/skills/topview-3d-cli/SKILL.md)).

The limit that matters most is motion. Characters hold **one of 121 static poses**. Cameras and figures can move only by keyframing their position and rotation, with no walk cycles and no limb animation ([CLI reference](https://github.com/topviewai/topview-3d-builder/blob/main/docs/topview-3d-cli.md)). Topview's own write-up says it would "rather state that plainly than market the project as a full 3D animation suite" ([Topview on DEV, via search snippet](https://dev.to/topviewai/from-one-prompt-to-an-editable-3d-scene-we-open-sourced-a-local-first-agent-workflow-npa)). It follows that the README's demos of a runner flipping or a dragon in flight get their body motion from the video model. You control where the action happens and where the camera sits, not the action itself.

### The handoff is a gray-box clip and a "change the look" prompt

Locally, Topview produces PNG frames, a contact sheet and a JSON manifest. To get a video, you export one from Studio, or the agent renders in batches of nine frames and joins them with ffmpeg ([generate.md](https://github.com/topviewai/topview-3d-builder/blob/main/skills/topview-3d-cli/references/generate.md)). **No depth maps, pose skeletons, camera-path files or 3D scene exports are documented**. So the output feeds video models that accept a reference video, not the depth- or pose-driven control models of the open-source world. By default the gray-box clip becomes `Video1` in a structured prompt that includes a "Reference lock" and the instruction "Lock layout, motion and camera; change the look." The prompt is written for "Seedance, MiniMax H3, Kling, Jimeng, Runway, Luma and similar" ([video-prompt.md](https://github.com/topviewai/topview-3d-builder/blob/main/skills/topview-3d-cli/references/video-prompt.md)). The agent uses a service you name first, then one already installed on your machine, and offers Topview only if it finds nothing. The Topview route signs you in through OAuth and spends credits. The agent must show you the model, prompt, duration and estimated cost before each call, and must "never purchase credits or create extra variants on your own" ([generate.md](https://github.com/topviewai/topview-3d-builder/blob/main/skills/topview-3d-cli/references/generate.md)). Third parties quote T1 at about **$0.22 per second** ([Alex Prompter on X](https://x.com/alex_prompter/status/2112965161573093743)). Topview plans reportedly run from $8 to $112 a month ([novoads.ai](https://novoads.ai/en/blog/topview-pricing)). Topview concedes the central weakness: "a reference frame can't guarantee that a video model will keep your layout" ([Topview on DEV, via search snippet](https://dev.to/topviewai/from-one-prompt-to-an-editable-3d-scene-we-open-sourced-a-local-first-agent-workflow-npa)).

Two practical details matter for a beginner. First, setup needs Python 3.11+, Node.js 20.6+ and uv or pipx. The README's suggested first step is to paste one sentence into your agent asking it to install the plugin ([README](https://github.com/topviewai/topview-3d-builder/blob/main/README.md)). Second, agent sandboxes can block the rendering browser even when the health check passes. You then have to approve running the render command outside the sandbox ([doctor-browser.md](https://github.com/topviewai/topview-3d-builder/blob/main/skills/topview-3d-cli/references/doctor-browser.md)). The README recommends Qwen 3.8 Flash as the agent model, at "about 1/150" the cost of GPT-6 Astra, and rates Claude Opus 5.5 three stars as "strong aesthetics, but expensive." Topview also thanks the Qwen team for "strong support," so read that rating as marketing ([README](https://github.com/topviewai/topview-3d-builder/blob/main/README.md)).

### Scenario and Blender MCP trade ease of use for real animation

Scenario's repo is the heavier option. It has two layers: skills that call Scenario's paid cloud service, and 87 "expert tool" skills that drive desktop 3D software and game engines (ZBrush, Blender, Maya, Unreal, Unity, Godot, Roblox Studio) installed on your machine ([Scenario README](https://github.com/scenario-labs/skills/blob/main/README.md)). The skill relevant to previs is `scenario-blender-blockout`. It turns a text brief or a reference image into a color-coded Blender blockout with a camera locked to the timeline, plus a hand-off sheet for the video prompt. Its stated principle is the cleanest statement of the whole approach: "the blockout has full authority over motion and layout and none over look" ([blockout SKILL.md](https://github.com/scenario-labs/skills/blob/main/skills/dcc/blender/scenario-blender-blockout/SKILL.md)). Unlike Topview, it **does animate figures**: rigged mannequins walk with planted feet ([Blender family README](https://github.com/scenario-labs/skills/blob/main/skills/dcc/blender/README.md)). The cost is that you need Blender 5.2 and some comfort with it.

General Blender MCP, a community plugin with about 33,000 stars that lets any AI model operate Blender, is the most flexible route. But it has no previs workflow or built-in checks ([mcp-for-blender](https://github.com/ahujasid/mcp-for-blender)). Creators' reports set expectations. Claude built a rough shot reference in two to three minutes ([workflow wiki](https://github.com/cheercheung/Awesome-Blender-Seedance-Workflow-Usecases/wiki)). In a Curious Refuge tutorial, wrists moved unnaturally and "Claude didn't add the camera movements we'd hoped to see," which took further rounds of feedback to fix ([Curious Refuge](https://curiousrefuge.com/blog/how-to-control-ai-videos-with-blender-and-claude)). Rendering 150 frames through the MCP connection tends to time out, so render in Blender's background mode instead ([note.com walkthrough](https://note.com/truenorthai/n/n9c6ef9b817f7?hl=en)). For someone who only wants to lock blocking and camera, Topview is the easiest of these. Scenario's blockout is the step up when figures need to walk.

## Code Video Model shows the gray box for only the first five steps

Code Video Model is a research system, not a product. It explains something the consumer tools only gesture at in their prompts: how to get a video model to obey a gray box without copying how the gray box looks.

### The problem: reference-video models copy the flat look

The authors' starting observation: "existing video models tend to copy the flat appearance of the proxy video into the generated clip". Here "proxy" means the gray-box render. Give a reference-video model the proxy and a prompt asking for photorealism, and it "returns the flat Three.js look, which the prompt cannot override" ([tech report](https://code-video-model.github.io/tech_report.pdf)). Their pipeline works around this in stages. First, GPT-6 Astra, running through the GitHub Copilot CLI at "xhigh" reasoning effort, writes a Three.js scene. Three.js is a library for drawing 3D scenes in a web browser. The agent must make at least four review passes over rendered keyframes and export a per-frame camera path ([program.md](https://github.com/code-video-model/CodeVideoModel/blob/main/prompts/program.md)). The proxy renders in under a minute. GPT-Image-2 then restyles its first frame into a photoreal reference image that keeps every object and the camera angle. Finally, the frozen MiniMax-H3 reference-to-video model generates a **5-second, 960×544 clip** ([tech report](https://code-video-model.github.io/tech_report.pdf), [README](https://github.com/code-video-model/CodeVideoModel)).

### The proxy steers 5 of 20 denoising steps, then disappears

A diffusion video model starts from pure static and removes noise over a series of passes, here 20. The earliest passes happen at the highest noise. They settle the coarse, blurry facts: where things are, how they move, where the camera goes. Later passes commit to edges, textures and lighting. Independent work supports this. A study of camera control in video models found camera motion is set within roughly the first 10% of the process ([AC3D](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2411.18673)), and image diffusion research shows coarse content forms at high noise ([Perception Prioritized Training](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.00227)). Code Video Model exploits this. It does not use SDEdit, which noises the guide video's own pixels and uses that as the starting point ([SDEdit](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.01073)). It does not use a ControlNet-style add-on that injects depth at every step. Instead it starts from pure noise and feeds the clean proxy through MiniMax-H3's built-in reference inputs, one through its vision-language encoder and one as compressed image data (VAE latents). It shows the proxy **only for the first 5 of 20 steps**, then deletes those inputs, and the remaining 15 steps see only the text prompt and the photoreal reference image ([video.py](https://github.com/code-video-model/CodeVideoModel/blob/main/cvm/video.py)). The code even tells the model to treat the reference image as appearance only: "Do not copy the image's exact composition, object identity, pose, or spatial layout." The authors measured how strongly each input affects the model's output at each step. The proxy's influence peaks in the earliest steps and fades continuously, which justifies the cutoff ([tech report §4.3](https://code-video-model.github.io/tech_report.pdf)).

The results show a trade-off, not a free win. Scores below come from a vision-language model acting as judge, rating 0–100 ([tech report Tables 1, 4](https://code-video-model.github.io/tech_report.pdf)):

| Method (same frozen H3 unless noted) | Overall | Structure | Style |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Code Video Model (proxy for first 5 steps)** | **85.17** | 81.98 | **80.33** |
| Proxy visible for all 20 steps | 75.22 | 87.67 | 57.07 |
| VACE with depth (Wan, trained control model) | 74.29 | 78.74 | 57.59 |
| SDEdit (noised proxy as starting point) | 64.37 | **93.92** | 10.34 |
| Text prompt only | 63.80 | — | — |

Approaches that maximize structure also copy the gray look. SDEdit keeps the layout almost perfectly but scores **10 for style**. Code Video Model gives up some structure to get the look back. The ablations sharpen the point. Moving the same five conditioned steps to the *end* scores 69.43, worse than using no proxy at all (71.32). Removing the photoreal reference image drops the score to 61.52, the lowest of the reported ablation scores ([tech report Tables 2, 6](https://code-video-model.github.io/tech_report.pdf)). In the blinded user study, Code Video Model won overall (8.17) but scored *lower* on structure (6.92) than SDEdit (8.64). The authors acknowledge it "may not reproduce every object boundary, pose, or spatial relation exactly." Edits to the reference image can also be overridden: blue recoloring "largely reverts to the warm daylight palette" ([tech report §4.3, §8](https://code-video-model.github.io/tech_report.pdf)). Weigh these numbers with caution. The benchmark has only 22 cases, built by the same agent pipeline. AI models do the judging, and GPT-6 is both the coding agent and one of the judges. The paper does not report how many human raters took part.

### You can't run this at home, and hosted services can't apply the schedule

Running the full system needs restricted-access GPT-6 Astra, reported at $10/$50 per million input/output tokens ([apiyi](https://docs.apiyi.com/en/news/gpt-6-astra-launch)). It also needs a MiniMax-H3 checkpoint whose verification step "reads about 210 GB" ([download_h3.py](https://github.com/code-video-model/CodeVideoModel/blob/main/download_h3.py)), and a default setup that splits work across **two CUDA GPUs** ([generate_video.py](https://github.com/code-video-model/CodeVideoModel/blob/main/inference/generate_video.py)). Secondary sources also report that the MiniMax H3 Community License excludes the US, EU, UK and South Korea from its covered territory ([WaveSpeed](https://wavespeed.ai/blog/minimax-h3/minimax-h3-community-license/)). Check that if you live in one of those places. The authors did run their pipeline through Seedance 2.5's public API in a demo, but they don't describe how. A hosted API exposes no per-step control ([tech report §6](https://code-video-model.github.io/tech_report.pdf)). As far as public documentation shows, a hosted reference-video mode uses the gray box for the whole generation, with at most an overall strength setting. In the research model's terms, that is the setup that copies the flat look most (style 57). Two practical lessons follow, and both are inferences from these results. First, always pair the gray-box clip with a restyled, photoreal first frame as the look reference, which is the input whose removal hurt most. Second, tell the model explicitly that the video is for layout and camera only.

### "Code runs the world, a model renders it" is a broader shift

A 3D scene can steer a video model in three ways. It can export signals such as depth or pose maps for trained control models, like VACE or Wan's Fun Control models, which run in ComfyUI ([Comfy Fun Control workflow](https://comfy.org/workflows/video_wan2_2_14B_fun_control-67a816af8a73/)). It can supply a camera path. Or it can hand over the raw gray-box video as a reference, which is the route Topview takes. The "code world" projects use trained renderers fed by engineered signals: StreamRender-H3 claims 218 ms renders on NVIDIA GB200 hardware ([Sana PR #533](https://github.com/NVlabs/Sana/pull/533)), EdenLive renders at 24 fps or more on an RTX 5090 ([EdenLive](https://github.com/VAST-AI-Research/EdenLive)), and Matrix-Game 4.0 reaches up to 67 FPS on an RTX 5080 rendering low-poly games ([Matrix-Game 4](https://github.com/SkyworkAI/Matrix-Game/tree/main/Matrix-Game-4)). ATAS tackles a different problem, keeping one shared 3D world consistent across many shots ([ATAS](https://github.com/ziplab/ATAS)). MotionBricks generates character skeleton motion at a claimed 15,000 FPS ([MotionBricks](https://github.com/NVlabs/GR00T-WholeBodyControl/tree/main/motionbricks)). That is exactly the piece Topview lacks, but nobody has connected the two yet.

## Start with references and an editor; use previs when shots fail on "where"

Previs-first is **not the best way to start**. It is a strong upgrade later on. What makes a beginner's video "cool" is mostly shot choice, pacing, sound and grade. Hosted tools teach those fastest, and they already give you a lot of control without any 3D. Kling 3.0 renders two to six connected shots in one generation ([fal.ai](https://fal.ai/learn/tools/seedance-2-0-vs-kling-3-0)). Seedance 2.0 officially "picks up camera movements from reference videos," and Dreamina's FAQ suggests labeling the reference's role, e.g., "Video 1 only for the slow camera movement" ([Dreamina Seedance](https://dreamina.capcut.com/tools/seedance-2-0), [Dreamina FAQ](https://dreamina.capcut.com/ai-video/ai-video-reference-inputs-faq)). That means filming a phone move can replace a 3D camera rig for many shots. Veo 3.1 adds reference images and first/last-frame control ([Google Developers Blog](https://developers.googleblog.com/introducing-veo-3-1-and-new-creative-capabilities-in-the-gemini-api/)). Runway's Aleph 2.0 edits existing footage (restyle, relight, swap elements) at 28 credits per second ([The Rundown](https://www.therundown.ai/tools/aleph)). Don't plan around Sora: secondary sources report the app was discontinued in April 2026 and its API retired in September 2026 ([invideo](https://invideo.io/blog/sora-ai-video-generator/)).

Previs earns its place where spatial relationships must hold. Runway's own previs guide says generative models have no persistent 3D space but "will typically hold blocking rendered from geometry you built." It also says exact spatial continuity between shots does not survive, and recommends moving precision shots into a 3D pipeline ([Runway](https://runway.com/resources/ai-previs)). Kling's own limitations post lists characters drifting, props disappearing and continuity breaking as sequences get longer ([Kling](https://kling.ai/blog/limitations-of-current-ai-video-generation-technology)). One creator's chain (Midjourney start frame, Blender blockout and camera, then Seedance) gave him "precise camera control," though he notes AI dialogue is still flat ([Reid Hannaford on X](https://x.com/reidhannaford/status/2071595581508563168)). Previs is overkill for single-subject shots, mood pieces, B-roll and most music videos, where the edit carries the work. No study has measured previs against prompting, so this guidance rests on vendor advice, one HCI paper and creator reports ([PrevizWhiz](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3772318.3790534)).

Topview does change one part of that equation: trying previs used to mean learning Blender. Now it means one install sentence to your agent and an afternoon. It pays off only if your generator accepts a reference video, such as Seedance's omni mode, Kling Omni, Aleph or Luma Modify. Otherwise it reduces to rendering a nice first frame. | 3. Structure without 3D | Multi-shot, first/last frames, reference-video camera transfer, editing existing clips with Aleph or Modify. Try a two-character scene and note what breaks | +1–3 weeks | +$15–35/mo if adding Runway or Luma |
| 4. Agent previs | Topview 3D Builder, then Scenario's Blender blockout if figures must walk. Feed the gray-box clip plus a restyled first frame to a reference-video model | +2–4 weeks | 3D stage free; agent subscription + generator |
| 5. Optional local control | ComfyUI with Wan VACE / Fun Control driven by depth or pose renders from your 3D scene | Several weeks | $0 per clip on a 16–24 GB GPU, or Comfy Cloud from $20/mo ([Comfy Cloud](https://comfy.org/cloud)) |

The rule for moving from stage 3 to stage 4: if a shot fails twice because of *where* things are (who stands where, screen direction, a camera path that must hit marks, geography across three or more shots), block it in 3D. If it fails because of *how it looks*, fix it with references, prompts or video-to-video editing instead.

## Use the MacBook as your main machine unless the PC has a big NVIDIA card

The path recommended above runs video generation in the cloud, so the machine matters mostly for four things: how smoothly the agent runs, previs, editing, and any local generation you add later.

For agents, macOS has fewer workarounds. Claude Code supports macOS 13+ and Windows 10 1809+. But its sandbox, which limits what the agent's commands can touch, runs on macOS, Linux and WSL2 (Linux inside Windows) and **not on native Windows**, where commands run unsandboxed ([Claude Code setup](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/setup), [sandboxing](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/sandboxing)). Native Windows also brings recurring friction with MCP tool connections: `cmd /c` wrappers, PATH not being inherited, and Git Bash path issues ([Blender MCP README](https://github.com/ahujasid/blender-mcp), [mcp.directory](https://mcp.directory/blog/claude-code-mcp-on-windows-native-wsl-2026-complete-fix-guide)). Topview documents install paths for macOS, Linux and Windows. Its renderer is a headless browser, so the 3D stage probably doesn't need a strong GPU on either machine, though Topview publishes no hardware spec ([doctor-browser.md](https://github.com/topviewai/topview-3d-builder/blob/main/skills/topview-3d-cli/references/doctor-browser.md)). Blender's high-quality renderer (Cycles) is far faster on desktop NVIDIA cards: an RTX 4090 scores about 13,064 in Blender's benchmark versus about 5,209 for an M4 Max ([Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/pc-components/gpus/rtx-5090-beats-rtx-4090-by-36-percent-in-the-blender-benchmark-rtx-5090d-is-40-percent-faster-than-rtx-4090d), [Club386](https://www.club386.com/apple-m4-max-chip-beats-nvidias-rtx-4070-in-blender)). Gray-box previs doesn't use Cycles, so this rarely matters. Unreal Engine on Mac lacks hardware ray tracing ([Epic](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/hardware-and-software-specifications-for-unreal-engine)). For editing, PugetBench results show M5 Pro and M5 Max MacBooks "clearly beat" RTX 5090 laptops in DaVinci Resolve and Adobe tests ([Notebookcheck](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Apple-s-M5-Pro-M5-Max-easily-beat-the-RTX-5090-in-PugetBench-creator-benchmarks.1252582.0.html)), and Final Cut Pro is Mac-only.

Local generation reverses the picture. Open video models are built for NVIDIA's CUDA. Wan 2.2's 5B model needs about 24 GB of VRAM at 720p and makes a 5-second clip in under nine minutes on one consumer GPU ([Wan2.2](https://github.com/Wan-Video/Wan2.2)). HunyuanVideo 1.5 needs at least 14 GB and officially supports only Linux ([HunyuanVideo-1.5](https://github.com/Tencent-Hunyuan/HunyuanVideo-1.5)). LTX's desktop app runs on Apple Silicon only in cloud-API mode ([LTX docs](https://docs.ltx.io/open-source-model/getting-started/quick-start)). On a Mac, the compressed FP8 model files that official templates use fail outright because Apple's Metal graphics framework has no FP8 support. One M1 Max took about **82 minutes for a 2-second Wan 2.2 clip** using compressed GGUF files ([lilting.ch](https://lilting.ch/en/articles/ltx2-wan22-mac-local-video-gen)). Neither machine can realistically run Code Video Model itself.

### What to check on each machine

On the PC, open a terminal and run `nvidia-smi`, or open Task Manager → Performance → GPU. Note the card's name and its "Dedicated GPU memory." Check whether it is a laptop variant, since laptop RTX cards are weaker than desktop cards with the same name. If it's AMD or Intel, ComfyUI Desktop doesn't support it ([comfyui-wiki](https://comfyui-wiki.com/en/install/install-comfyui/comfyui-desktop-installation-guide)). Under Settings → System → About, note the system RAM and the Windows version, which needs to be 10 1809 or newer. Check free SSD space too: LTX-2's checkpoints alone take about 43 GB ([localaimaster](https://localaimaster.com/blog/ltx-2-local-setup-guide)). On the Mac, open Apple menu → About This Mac and note the chip (M-series versus Intel), the memory and the macOS version, which needs to be 13 or newer. For the GPU core count, run `system_profiler SPDisplaysDataType` in Terminal. An Intel Mac can't use ComfyUI Desktop ([ComfyUI FAQ](https://docs.comfy.org/installation/desktop/faq)).

| What you find | What each machine should do |
|---|---|
| PC: NVIDIA, **16 GB+ VRAM**, 32 GB+ RAM | PC becomes your local render box: ComfyUI with Wan (16 GB is the floor for the 14B model at 720p, 20 GB is comfortable ([localaimaster](https://localaimaster.com/blog/wan-vram-requirements-by-gpu))) and LTX (16 GB+ ([LTX docs](https://docs.ltx.io/open-source-model/getting-started/quick-start))). Mac stays the agent and editing machine |
| PC: NVIDIA, 8–12 GB | Only compressed models at 480p (12 GB for the 14B model ([localaimaster](https://localaimaster.com/blog/wan-vram-requirements-by-gpu))). Use cloud models for final shots |
| PC: AMD/Intel graphics or under 8 GB | No useful local video. Use the Mac for everything |
| Mac: Apple Silicon, any memory | Primary machine: agent, Topview or Blender previs, cloud generation, editing |
| Mac: Apple Silicon, 32 GB+ | The same, plus slow local experiments (32 GB is the realistic floor ([Earngenix](https://www.earngenix.com/tutorials/vram-hardware-guide-ai-video-comfyui))) |
| Mac: Intel | Use the PC as primary if it's reasonably modern. Otherwise stay cloud-only |

If you end up using the PC for agent work, choose between two setups. Run Claude Code natively with Git for Windows when it needs to drive Windows apps such as Blender or ComfyUI, and accept that there is no sandbox. Or run it inside WSL2 for the sandbox and Linux tooling ([Claude Code setup](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/setup)).

## Conclusion

What lasts here is the division of labor, not any one tool. A program decides geometry, camera and timing; a model decides appearance. Consumer skills and research systems are adopting that split from opposite ends. The gray-box clip is portable: it works with Topview, Blender or Three.js on one side and with Seedance, Kling or H3 on the other. So learning to block a shot builds skills that last, while betting on a particular 3D tool does not. The real bottleneck sits downstream, in how faithfully a video model follows a reference video without copying its look. Code Video Model shows this can be solved at the level of the generation steps. Hosted services don't yet expose that control, so previs today steers the video model without binding it.

That points to a practical sequence. Spend your first month on stories, sound and editing in hosted tools, where your results improve fastest. By the time your ideas need two characters to hold their marks across five shots, the previs tools will likely have matured. Three signs it's time to move: hosted services add a structure-only reference control, Topview adds body animation (MotionBricks-style motion is the obvious candidate), or real-time renderers like EdenLive become easy to install. Until then, treat Topview as a cheap experiment worth trying once you reach stage 3. Don't build your whole workflow on it yet.
